The Delegated Email Detection That Misses Most Delegated Email

Almost every detection library contains a rule for mail sent under another identity. The logic is the same everywhere. Look for SendAs and SendOnBehalf events in the Exchange audit log, compare the acting account against the mailbox owner, and alert when they differ.

WHERE Operation IN ('SendAs','SendOnBehalf')
  AND UserId != MailboxOwnerUPN

It is a reasonable rule. It is also, in a large proportion of cases, blind to the behaviour it was written to catch.

While baselining delegated sending in a Microsoft 365 tenant, we traced a member of staff through several hours of ordinary casework. She held delegated access to two shared mailboxes and used it constantly. She opened a message in a colleague's mailbox, composed a reply, and sent it on that colleague's behalf. That is exactly what the rule exists to see.

The rule saw none of it.

The reason is in how Exchange records the event. When the reply is composed in Outlook on the web from a message opened in a delegated mailbox, the SendOnBehalf event is written with the acting user as both the actor and the mailbox owner. UserId and MailboxOwnerUPN are identical. The delegated relationship is recorded elsewhere in the event, in SendOnBehalfOfUserSmtp, but the two fields the rule compares are the same value, so the comparison fails and nothing is reported.

The same person's sends were captured on some occasions and missed on others, depending on where the message was composed rather than on what she was doing. That is the worst kind of detection gap, because it is not consistent enough to be noticed and not random enough to be dismissed.

Why This Matters

Mail sent under a colleague's identity is one of the more consequential things an attacker does with a compromised mailbox. It is the mechanism behind invoice fraud, payment redirection and internal phishing, because a message that appears to come from a trusted colleague clears the recipient's judgement in a way an external message never does.

A rule that catches this behaviour only in certain client paths is not a control. It is a source of false confidence.

The Fix

Do not compare the actor to the mailbox owner. Read the delegation field directly.

  • SendAsUserSmtp
  • SendOnBehalfOfUserSmtp

These are populated on the event whenever a delegated send occurs, regardless of which client composed it. Compare the acting account against that value instead:

WHERE Operation IN ('SendAs','SendOnBehalf')
  AND lower(UserId) != lower(coalesce(
          nullIf(SendAsUserSmtp,''), SendOnBehalfOfUserSmtp))

That covers both paths. It also removes a class of false negative that is extremely difficult to find by inspection, because the rule appears to work: it returns results, the results are correct, and nothing indicates that a larger set of equally valid results never appeared.

A Second Problem Worth Anticipating

Once the rule catches everything it should, a new difficulty appears in organizations that operate shared mailboxes. Delegated sending is not an anomaly in a service organization. It is the operating model. Reception sends for the practice, an intake mailbox sends for a coordinator, an account handles brokerage correspondence. Alerting on every instance produces a case per message and teaches the analyst to close them without reading.

The discriminator is not the act. It is novelty.

An account sending under an identity it has used for months is business as usual. An account sending under an identity it has never used before is the event worth one question: has this person been given delegated access, or is this an account under somebody else's control writing as a colleague?

That reduces a rule which would otherwise fire many times a day to one that fires when a new delegation appears, and then stays quiet.

What To Check In Your Own Tenant

Run a thirty day aggregation of SendAs and SendOnBehalf grouped by actor and by the delegation field, then run the same aggregation grouped by actor and mailbox owner. If the two sets differ, your detection is missing the difference.

We would encourage verifying this in your own environment rather than taking our word for it. Audit schema behaviour varies between tenants and between client versions, and the observation above comes from a limited number of tenants rather than from documented Microsoft behaviour. The check takes a few minutes and the answer is unambiguous.

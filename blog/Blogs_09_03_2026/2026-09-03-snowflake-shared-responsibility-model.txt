<h2>What is the shared responsibility model in managed security for Snowflake?</h2>
<p>The shared responsibility model in managed security for Snowflake dictates that Snowflake is responsible for the security <em>of</em> the cloud (infrastructure, hardware, and core software), while the customer is responsible for security <em>in</em> the cloud, including data governance, user access management, and configuration of security policies.</p>

<p>This division ensures that while the foundation is secure, the specific implementation—such as who has access to sensitive tables or how authentication is enforced—remains under the customer’s control. When you engage a managed security provider, you are essentially outsourcing the monitoring and response of your specific "side" of this model.</p>

<h2 id="how-it-works">How does the shared responsibility model in managed security for Snowflake work?</h2>
<p>The model functions as a clear boundary line. Snowflake maintains the "Platform Security," which includes physical data center security, host OS security, and physical network security. You, the customer, maintain the "Data Security" and "Access Security."</p>

<p>When you add a managed security service, they act as an extension of your internal team. They don't take over Snowflake’s infrastructure responsibilities; instead, they ingest your logs, monitor your access patterns, and alert you to suspicious activity that occurs within your own account configurations. This partnership ensures that the "customer side" of the responsibility is actively managed around the clock.</p>

<h2 id="implementation">How do you implement the shared responsibility model in managed security for Snowflake?</h2>
<h3>Step 1: Audit your current access controls</h3>
<p>Begin by reviewing all existing users, roles, and permissions within your Snowflake environment. Identify any accounts with excessive privileges or inactive users that should be de-provisioned.</p>

<h3>Step 2: Implement Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)</h3>
<p>Enforcing MFA is the single most effective way to secure your side of the responsibility model. Ensure that every user, especially those with administrative roles, is required to use MFA for every login attempt.</p>

<h3>Step 3: Centralize and monitor logs</h3>
<p>Snowflake provides comprehensive access history and query history logs. Use these to feed a security monitoring platform like Vigilense AI, which specializes in detecting anomalies that occur within your data cloud.</p>

<h3>Step 4: Establish a response playbook</h3>
<p>Define clear procedures for what happens when a threat is detected. Who is contacted? How is the account isolated? A plan ensures that you are ready to act when an alert triggers.</p>

<h3>Step 5: Continuously review and refine</h3>
<p>Security is not a one-time setup. Regularly review your logs and threat reports to adjust your security policies as your business needs evolve and new threat vectors emerge.</p>

<h2 id="mistakes">What are common shared responsibility model mistakes?</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Assuming "Cloud-Native" means "Secure":</strong> Believing that using a secure platform automatically makes your data secure without your own configurations.</li>
<li><strong>Neglecting IAM:</strong> Failing to implement the principle of least privilege, leading to accounts with broad data access.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring Log Data:</strong> Letting Snowflake logs sit unmonitored, which means breaches go unnoticed for months.</li>
<li><strong>Static Security Policies:</strong> Failing to update security configurations as new users join or leave the organization.</li>
<li><strong>Lack of Incident Response:</strong> Having a detection mechanism but no process or team to investigate and mitigate the alerts.</li>
</ul>
